Meeting Lisa Su at RPI's Fireside Chat

AMD Semiconductors RPI Career

During my final year at RPI, I got to attend a fireside chat with Dr. Lisa Su, Chair and CEO of AMD, hosted by President Marty Schmidt during commencement weekend. Schmidt was her advisor at MIT, which made the whole conversation feel less like a PR appearance and more like two people who actually know each other talking through a career. He mentioned he'd downloaded her PhD thesis a couple nights before just to reread it.

What a PhD actually gives you

Her thesis was on SOI (Silicon on Insulator) devices. If you are unfamiliar, SOI is a transistor architecture where the silicon layer sits on top of an insulating oxide layer instead of the bulk substrate. It gives you better isolation, lower parasitic capacitance, and reduced leakage. All things that matter a lot at small geometries. At the time she was working on it in the early 1990s, it wasn't obvious SOI would go anywhere. It was a research topic that could have stayed academic. It didn't. Many modern high-performance devices are built on that foundation. IBM used SOI extensively in their PowerPC and later server chips.

But she made a point of saying the specific topic almost doesn't matter. What a PhD gives you is the experience of going so deep on something that you become the person who has to figure it out from scratch. Nobody hands you the answer. You have to solve a problem that, by definition, hasn't been solved before.

"You get an opportunity to really work on a topic and go very deep on a topic that perhaps is something that nobody else in the world is working on... and that gives you a tremendous amount of confidence in understanding that you can do some very original research. And that stays with you for your entire career."

She also said something that surprised me about academia: she described it as genuinely difficult in a way that stopped her from pursuing a professorship. Not because of the research itself, but because you have to always be finding the next groundbreaking thing. That relentless horizon of original discovery is what academia demands. She had huge respect for it. Her parents wanted her to become a professor. But she realized her instinct was to build products on a team, not hunt for the next frontier problem indefinitely. Knowing the difference between those two modes is something a lot of people don't figure out until much later.

IBM: two stories worth remembering

After a short stint at Texas Instruments (she moved to Dallas at 24 knowing nobody, decided it wasn't for her, moved back to New York), she joined IBM. John Kelly, who was VP of semiconductors at the time, became one of her most important mentors. She described getting called into his very large office as a fairly new employee and being asked what she wanted to do when she grew up. She said she wasn't sure, but there were two options he laid out: become an IBM Fellow (she immediately ruled that out because that was for people like Bob Dennard, who wrote the foundational scaling laws for MOSFETs) or become an IBM VP. She thought she could probably do that.

About five years in, she got assigned as Lou Gerstner's technical assistant. Gerstner was the CEO who saved IBM in the 90s and came from a consulting background, not an engineering one. Her job was to keep him up to date on technical topics, translating device physics and speeds-and-feeds into something a business leader could actually use to make decisions. She described it as an eye-opening nine months. You realize at that level the questions aren't about the technology itself but about how technology changes what's possible in the world.

She told two stories from that time. One: Napster had just come out, and she'd spend time with Gerstner showing him how you could download music from the internet. He was immediately concerned about copyright. Two: IBM had an active speech recognition research program, and she set up voice recognition in his car, recorded his voice, trained it, the whole thing. Sent it home with him. A week later she asked how it went. He told her: "I didn't have the heart to tell you. It absolutely did not work." Her read on it: tech sounds great until it actually has to work. That gap between demo and reliable product is where most of the real engineering lives.

The other IBM story that stuck with me was about copper interconnects. She was put in charge of bringing copper wiring into the CMOS process to replace aluminum, which had been the standard. Copper has lower resistivity, which means faster signals and less power dissipation, but copper contaminates silicon and the fab processes at the time weren't built for it. Early yield was very very low. She was ready to move on to something new once the discovery phase was done. Kelly pushed back: stay through manufacturing. That's where you learn the most. She did, and said he was right. Going from building something once to building it reliably millions of times is its own form of engineering that most people skip.

Two more from IBM

When she moved from engineering into running her first business unit at IBM, she committed to a revenue plan by doing the obvious thing: she took everything her sales team told her, added it up, and submitted that as the number. Two months into the year she realized she was going to miss it by 50%. Her instinct in the moment was to push back on the sales team. She went home that night, thought about it, and came in the next day knowing it was completely on her. She'd never triangulated the forecast or pressure-tested any of it. That was the lesson.

The other one was about managing people for the first time. She had a team of about ten engineers. Her boss pulled her aside and asked: do you ever talk to your people? She said of course, she talked to them constantly. He asked again. She genuinely didn't understand what he meant. What he meant was: do you ever ask how they're doing? Do they enjoy the work? She had never done that, because she personally didn't like being asked those things and had assumed everyone was the same way. It took someone pointing it out directly for her to realize that wasn't how people worked.

The chiplet bet and AMD's turnaround

When she became CEO of AMD in 2014, the company was at roughly 8,000 people and had missed several product cycles. People outside suggested she chase smartphones and tablets since that's where consumer momentum was. She didn't. AMD was good at building big, high-performance processors, not mobile, and she thought it was more important to be excellent at one thing than mediocre at many.

The core technical bet was chiplets: instead of one large monolithic die, cut the design into smaller chips and connect them on a package substrate. Moore's law scaling was slowing down, so brute-force transistor shrinks weren't delivering the same gains anymore. Chiplets gave you another axis to work on. Yield improves when dies are smaller, and you can mix process nodes for different parts of the chip. She gathered AMD's fellows, sat down, and asked: are we sure we want to make this bet? It was a roadmap-defining call. They said yes. That architecture is now industry standard. Everybody uses chiplets for high-performance processors.

The decisions you make today impact results three to five years from now in hardware. Chiplets looked like a risky gamble in 2015. By 2020 it was the reason AMD's Zen CPUs were competitive. That five-year lag is just the physics of the job.

One concrete sign that domestic manufacturing is actually working: AMD was among the first customers through TSMC's new fab in Arizona, and she said the yields coming out of Arizona are equivalent to what comes out of Taiwan. Not close. Equivalent. That's not a given. It's an argument that reshoring is possible, it just takes time to get there.

On AI

She called AI the most transformational technology she's seen in her career. Her framing was specific: you're taking computing and making every application more intelligent. We're about two years into what she thinks is a 10-year cycle. AMD is building AI into every part of their stack, data center GPUs, consumer products, edge devices, and using it internally to help design the next generation of chips.

When asked what the industry looks like in 10 years, she said she wants computing to be so easy you don't even think about it. We're not there yet. Her example: ChatGPT wasn't new AI, AI had been around for a long time. What it did was remove a layer of friction. You could just talk to it. That's the direction she thinks everything should move, intelligence that adds value without requiring you to think about the technology underneath it.

On power, she didn't sugarcoat it. Data centers are already being built at multiple gigawatts and that's going to keep growing. More compute means more intelligence, and the demand for both isn't slowing down. Every country is scrambling to add power capacity and she said the US needs to do the same.

On DeepSeek she was direct. The initial reaction, that China had somehow leapt ahead, was the wrong interpretation. DeepSeek is a good model built on techniques the US frontier labs developed. But what it showed is the world isn't standing still. Necessity is the mother of invention, and open-source models spread fast. Her position, which she'd just come from testifying about in front of the Senate Commerce Committee: the US leads in AI today, that's a good position, but we have to run faster. Treating a lead as permanent is how you lose it.

After the talk

I got to meet her briefly after the Q&A. She was relaxed, asked real questions, didn't rush. The top two supercomputers in the world, Frontier at Oak Ridge National Lab and El Capitan at Lawrence Livermore, both run on AMD. That detail is a little surreal to think about standing next to someone.

She mentioned her mom once, briefly. Came to the US as an immigrant, started a business by herself, grew it into a multi-million dollar company. That's where the "you can do anything you put your mind to" came from, and it landed differently knowing the specific context behind it.

The thing I keep coming back to: she described academia as the place where you learn to solve problems that haven't been solved before. That's the capability a PhD is actually building. Whether you go into industry or research after doesn't change the fact that you spent years developing the instinct to find your own way to an answer that doesn't exist yet. That framing is more useful to me right now than most career advice I've heard, and it makes me consider pursuing one.
