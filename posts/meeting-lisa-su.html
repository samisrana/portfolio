Meeting Lisa Su at RPI's Fireside Chat

AMD Semiconductors RPI Career

During my final year at RPI, I had the incredible opportunity to attend a fireside chat with Dr. Lisa Su, the Chair and CEO of AMD, hosted by RPI President Marty Schmidt as part of commencement weekend. As someone passionate about semiconductors and computer architecture, this was an experience I'll never forget.

The Conversation

The discussion covered an impressive range of topics - from Dr. Su's journey as an immigrant from Taiwan who came to the US at age three, to her time at MIT where she discovered her love for building things in the clean room. What struck me most was her emphasis on being "best at classes that had lots of hands-on work" - something that resonated deeply with my own experience at RPI.

"Education is not job training. You're going to continue to evolve over the arc of your career in terms of learning new things."

Dr. Su shared fascinating stories from her career, including her time as Lou Gerstner's technical assistant at IBM, where she learned to translate deep technology into business impact. She candidly discussed the early days at AMD, when the company was struggling and people questioned whether she really wanted to take on the challenge of competing with Intel.

Key Insights on AI and the Future

One of the most compelling parts of the talk was her perspective on AI. She described it as "the most transformational technology" she's seen in her career, and we're only about two years into what she sees as a 10-year cycle. The implications for computing are massive - AMD is building AI capabilities into every part of their product portfolio.

Dr. Su emphasized that the world needs more compute, and there are very few companies that build it. This isn't a zero-sum game between competitors - the demand is enormous and growing.

She also discussed AMD's bet on chiplets - the idea of cutting up big chips into smaller pieces and connecting them on packages. At the time it was a risky move, but now the entire industry is following suit. It's a reminder that sometimes you have to make bold technology bets to leapfrog the competition.

On Leadership and Failure

When asked about failure, Dr. Su shared a story about her first business role at IBM. She committed to a revenue plan based on what her sales team told her, only to realize two months in she would miss it by 50%. Her initial instinct was to blame the sales team, but she went home that night and realized it was her responsibility. The lesson: own your mistakes and learn from them.

"The opportunity to lead people is incredibly rewarding because the idea of leadership is you want to make your team and every person on your team better than they thought they could be."

Meeting Her in Person

After the talk, I had the chance to meet Dr. Su briefly. It was surreal to shake hands with someone whose chips power some of the world's most powerful supercomputers - including the top two, at Oak Ridge and Lawrence Livermore National Labs. She was genuinely warm and took time to speak with students despite her busy schedule.

For anyone studying electrical engineering or computer science, seeing someone at the absolute pinnacle of the semiconductor industry take time to inspire the next generation was incredibly motivating. It reinforced my passion for working at the intersection of hardware and software, and reminded me that the best leaders never stop learning and never stop building.

Takeaways

  • Hands-on experience matters more than perfect grades
  • Take bold technology bets when you believe in them
  • Own your mistakes - they're your best learning opportunities
  • The semiconductor industry needs more people, and there's room for everyone
  • Pressure is actually a good thing - it brings out the best in people

This experience solidified my decision to pursue a career in embedded systems and hardware. If you ever get the chance to attend a talk by a leader you admire, take it. You never know how it might shape your path forward.
